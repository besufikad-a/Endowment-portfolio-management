Week 1Risk, Return & Asset Pricing Foundations
TheoryQuantitative

1.1 — Expected Return

The expected return is the probability-weighted average of all possible outcomes. It is the single most important input to portfolio construction because every allocation decision ultimately asks: "What do I expect to earn?" In practice, you rarely know true probabilities. Instead, you estimate expected returns from historical data (average past returns), forward-looking models (CAPM, factor models), or economic forecasts. Each approach has trade-offs — historical data is backward-looking, models depend on assumptions, and forecasts are subjective.

FormulaE(R) = Σ pᵢ × Rᵢ — where pᵢ = probability of state i, Rᵢ = return in state i
Real-World Application

An endowment CIO estimates three scenarios for US equities: Bull (30%, +18%), Base (50%, +8%), Bear (20%, −12%).

E(R) = 0.30×18% + 0.50×8% + 0.20×(−12%) = 5.4 + 4.0 − 2.4 = 7.0%

This 7% becomes the building block for deciding how much of the endowment to allocate to US equities vs. bonds or alternatives.

1.2 — Variance, Standard Deviation & Volatility

Variance measures the dispersion of returns around the expected value. Standard deviation (the square root of variance) is quoted as "volatility" — the most common risk measure. A stock with 20% annualized volatility has roughly a two-thirds chance of returning within ±20% of its expected return in a given year. Two portfolios can have the same expected return but vastly different volatilities. Endowments care deeply because high volatility can force spending cuts or require emergency liquidity.

FormulasVar(R) = σ² = Σ pᵢ × (Rᵢ − E(R))²
StdDev(R) = σ = √(σ²)
From sample data: σ² = (1/(n−1)) × Σ(Rₜ − R̄)²
Application

Using the scenario above: deviations from 7% are +11%, +1%, −19%.

σ² = 0.30(11%)² + 0.50(1%)² + 0.20(−19%)² = 36.3 + 0.5 + 72.2 = 109%²

σ = √109 ≈ 10.4% annualized volatility.

1.3 — Risk Premium & Risk-Free Rate

The risk premium is the extra return demanded for bearing risk above T-bills. Historically, the US equity risk premium has averaged ~5–7%/year. This number drives everything — a higher assumed ERP means heavier equity allocation. When real rates are negative (2020–2021), endowments are forced into riskier assets to meet spending targets.

FormulaRisk Premium = E(R) − Rf

1.4 — The Sharpe Ratio

The Sharpe ratio measures return per unit of total risk — the single most common institutional performance metric. A Sharpe of 0.5 means 0.5% excess return per 1% volatility. Typical equity portfolios achieve 0.3–0.5; exceptional managers sustain 1.0+.

FormulaSharpe = (E(Rₚ) − Rf) / σₚ
Endowment Example

Yale returned ~11.4% annualized over 20 years with ~12% vol. Rf averaged ~2%. Sharpe = (11.4−2.0)/12.0 = 0.78 — significantly above a 60/40 portfolio's ~0.45.

1.5 — Statistics Foundations

Mean vs. Median: Mean is sensitive to outliers; median gives the "typical" month. Both matter for fat-tailed return distributions.

Annualization: Monthly return annualizes as (1+Rₘ)¹²−1 (geometric) or Rₘ×12 (arithmetic). Monthly vol annualizes by ×√12. Getting this wrong is a common intern mistake.

Time Series: Returns have structure — serial correlation (momentum/mean reversion), volatility clustering (calm then storms), regime changes. Naively averaging 2009–2019 returns and applying to 2020 misses the COVID crash.

Data Quality: Survivorship bias (only surviving funds, overstating returns), backfill bias (managers report after good performance), stale pricing (illiquid assets appear less volatile). All pervasive in institutional data.

Week 2Asset Allocation & Mean-Variance Analysis
TheoryQuantitativeEndowment

2.1 — Covariance & Correlation

Covariance measures how two assets move together. Correlation standardizes it to −1 to +1. This is the engine behind diversification: combining assets with ρ < 1 makes portfolio volatility less than the weighted average of individual volatilities.

FormulasCov(A,B) = Σ pᵢ(Rₐᵢ−E(Rₐ))(Rᵦᵢ−E(Rᵦ))
ρ(A,B) = Cov(A,B)/(σₐ×σᵦ)

Two-asset portfolio variance:
σₚ² = w²ₐσ²ₐ + w²ᵦσ²ᵦ + 2wₐwᵦCov(A,B)
Endowment Application

US equities (σ=16%) and Treasuries (σ=6%), ρ=−0.2. A 60/40 portfolio:

σₚ² = 0.36(256) + 0.16(36) + 2(0.6)(0.4)(−0.2)(16)(6) = 92.16 + 5.76 − 4.61 = 93.31

σₚ = 9.66%. Weighted average would be 12.0%. Diversification saved 2.34% volatility — the "free lunch."

2.2 — The Efficient Frontier

The efficient frontier is the set of portfolios delivering maximum expected return for each risk level. Markowitz's 1952 insight: evaluate portfolios holistically, not individual securities. Risk depends on correlations, not just individual volatilities. In practice, the frontier is highly sensitive to input assumptions — small changes in expected returns dramatically shift optimal weights (the "Markowitz curse").

2.3 — Mean-Variance Optimization

MVO minimizes portfolio variance for a target return. Inputs: expected return vector (μ), covariance matrix (Σ), constraints. The tangency portfolio (where the CAL touches the frontier) maximizes the Sharpe ratio.

OptimizationMinimize: w'Σw (portfolio variance)
Subject to: w'μ = target return, w'𝟏 = 1

Capital Allocation Line: E(Rₚ) = Rf + [(E(Rₜ)−Rf)/σₜ] × σₚ

2.4 — Endowment Asset Allocation

The Harvard Management Co. case shows a real endowment allocating across 11+ asset classes, balancing return generation against a 5% spending requirement. The "policy portfolio" — the long-run strategic allocation — is the single most important investment decision, explaining 90%+ of return variation over time.

Why This Matters

When the investment committee debates moving 5% from domestic equity to private credit, they're having a mean-variance conversation: "What does this do to expected return and risk?" Understanding this framework lets you participate meaningfully.

Week 3CAPM, Beta & Risk Decomposition
TheoryApplied

3.1 — The Capital Asset Pricing Model

CAPM: expected return equals risk-free rate plus a premium proportional to beta. The key insight — only systematic risk is compensated because idiosyncratic risk diversifies away. Beta=1.0 means lockstep with the market; 1.5 means 50% more volatile; negative beta (rare) is a natural hedge.

CAPME(Rᵢ) = Rf + βᵢ × [E(Rₘ) − Rf]
βᵢ = Cov(Rᵢ,Rₘ) / Var(Rₘ)

Security Market Line: All assets should plot on E(R) = Rf + β×ERP
Above SML → positive alpha (underpriced)
Below SML → negative alpha (overpriced)

3.2 — Risk Decomposition

Total risk = systematic (β²σ²ₘ) + idiosyncratic (σ²ε). R² = fraction explained by the market. If R²=0.85, 85% of the fund's risk is systematic — endowments shouldn't pay active fees for that; they can get beta cheaply via index funds.

Decompositionσ²ᵢ = β²ᵢσ²ₘ + σ²ε
R² = β²ᵢσ²ₘ / σ²ᵢ
Application

Manager with β=1.1, σ=20%, market σₘ=15%. Systematic=1.21×225=272.25. Total=400. Idiosyncratic=127.75. R²=68%. Only 68% is market risk — this is a reasonably active manager worth paying for if alpha is positive.

3.3 — Alpha

Alpha is return above what the risk model predicts — genuine skill. It's the primary measure endowments use to evaluate managers.

Jensen's Alphaαᵢ = Rᵢ − [Rf + βᵢ(Rₘ − Rf)]
Regression: Rᵢ−Rf = α + β(Rₘ−Rf) + ε

3.4 — Tracking Error & Information Ratio

Tracking error = σ(Rₚ−Rᵦ) — how much active risk a manager takes. Typical: 2–6%. Information Ratio = α/TE. IR > 0.5 is good; > 1.0 is exceptional.

FormulasTE = StdDev(Rₚ − Rᵦ)
Information Ratio = α / TE

Week 4Multifactor Models, APT & Equity Analysis
TheoryQuantitative

4.1 — Beyond CAPM

Beta alone cannot explain the cross-section of returns. Small-caps earn more than large-caps (size effect), cheap stocks outperform (value), and past winners keep winning (momentum). These anomalies led to multifactor models.

4.2 — Fama-French & Extensions

Fama-French (1993): expected returns are driven by market (MKT), size (SMB = Small Minus Big), and value (HML = High Minus Low). This explains far more than CAPM alone.

Fama-French 3-FactorRᵢ−Rf = α + β₁(Rₘ−Rf) + β₂(SMB) + β₃(HML) + ε

+ β₄(UMD) → Carhart 4-factor (momentum)
+ β₅(RMW) + β₆(CMA) → Fama-French 5-factor
Alpha Decomposition

Endowment equity composite returned 14% vs. market 10%. Before declaring 4% alpha, run FF regression: β_MKT=1.05, β_SMB=0.4, β_HML=0.3. Factor returns: MKT=8%, SMB=3%, HML=5%.

Expected = 2% + 1.05(8%) + 0.4(3%) + 0.3(5%) = 13.1%. True α = 14% − 13.1% = 0.9%. Most "alpha" was cheap factor exposure.

4.3 — APT

Arbitrage Pricing Theory is more general than CAPM: expected returns are a linear function of multiple systematic factors, without specifying which. Only requires no-arbitrage conditions.

4.4 — Equity Fundamentals

Market Cap: Price × shares. Large (>$10B), Mid ($2–10B), Small (<$2B). Valuation: P/E (price/earnings, avg ~18–22×), P/B (book value), EV/EBITDA (capital-structure neutral). Style Factors: Growth (high rev growth, high P/E) vs. Value (low P/E, high yield). Plus momentum, quality, size. Endowments structure equity by style/factor buckets.

Exercises — Week 4

  2. Endowment equity composite: β_MKT=1.0, β_SMB=0.25, β_HML=−0.15, β_UMD=0.10. CIO wants aggregate β_HML=0.1. What factor trade achieves this?
  3. Pull current P/E for S&P 500, MSCI EAFE, MSCI EM. Which is cheapest? Why might low P/E ≠ "cheap" in EM?
  4. If an endowment can get value exposure via ETF at 15bps, why pay 75bps for an active value manager? When is it worth it?

Week 5Market Efficiency, Behavioral Finance & Performance Evaluation
TheoryAppliedEndowment

5.1 — Efficient Market Hypothesis

EMH: prices reflect all available information. Weak (past prices useless), Semi-strong (all public info priced), Strong (all info including insider). Implication for endowments: if efficient, active management destroys value — just index. Swensen's counter: efficiency varies by market. Large-cap US is highly efficient; PE and VC are not. Go passive where efficient, active where not.

5.2 — Behavioral Finance

Systematic investor deviations: overconfidence, loss aversion (losses hurt 2× gains), anchoring, herding (bubbles), disposition effect (sell winners early, hold losers). For endowments, biases affect both the investment committee (resisting rebalancing into crashes) and managers. Disciplined governance is the defense.

5.3 — Performance Measurement

TWR vs. MWR/IRR

TWR removes cash flow effects — measures pure manager skill. MWR/IRR captures the investor's actual experience including timing of flows. Both matter: TWR for manager evaluation, MWR for "did the endowment actually make money?"

TWR vs. MWRTWR: (1+TWR) = (1+r₁)(1+r₂)...(1+rₙ)
MWR/IRR: Solve for r in Σ CFₜ/(1+r)ᵗ = 0
The Timing Trap

Fund: +20% Year 1, −10% Year 2. TWR = (1.20)(0.90)−1 = 8.0%.

Investor puts $100 at start, adds $200 after Year 1 gain (total $320). After Year 2: $320×0.90 = $288. Invested $300, got $288. MWR ≈ −4%. Same manager, opposite investor experience.

Performance Attribution

Decompose return into: Allocation effect (over/underweighting classes), Selection effect (picking winners within classes), Interaction. Every quarterly IC meeting asks: "Did we add value by tilting toward PE (allocation) or by picking the right PE managers (selection)?"

Risk-Adjusted Metrics

Sharpe = excess return/total vol (standalone). Information Ratio = α/TE (benchmarked managers). Sortino = excess return/downside deviation (only harmful vol). Treynor = excess return/β (systematic risk).

Exercises — Week 5

  2. Policy: 60% equity/40% bonds. Actual: 70%/30%. Equity returned 12% (bench 10%), bonds 3% (bench 4%). Decompose into allocation and selection effects.
  3. Manager A: α=2%, TE=3%. Manager B: α=3%, TE=8%. Manager C: α=1.5%, TE=2%. Rank by IR. Which do you hire?
  4. IC member says: "Our PE portfolio returned 15% with 8% vol — best risk-adjusted class." Write a memo explaining why this is misleading (smoothing, IRR vs. TWR, vintage effects).

Week 6Fixed Income, Term Structure & Advanced Risk
TheoryQuantitative

6.1 — Bond Pricing & Yield

A bond's price = present value of all future cash flows discounted at YTM. YTM is the bond's IRR. When yields rise, prices fall — the most fundamental FI relationship.

Bond PriceP = Σ[C/(1+y)ᵗ] + F/(1+y)ⁿ — C=coupon, y=YTM/2 (semiannual), F=face

6.2 — Duration

Macaulay Duration: weighted-average time to receive cash flows. Modified Duration: % price change per 1% yield change. A bond with modified duration 7 loses ~7% if yields rise 1%. For endowments, FI duration should match the effective duration of spending obligations.

DurationMacaulay D = Σ[t × PV(CFₜ)] / Price
Modified D = Macaulay D / (1 + y/m)
ΔP/P ≈ −Modified D × Δy

6.3 — Convexity

Duration is linear; the actual price-yield relationship is curved. Convexity captures the curvature — positive convexity means gains from falling rates exceed losses from rising rates. Endowments seek positive convexity.

With ConvexityΔP/P ≈ −D×Δy + ½×Convexity×(Δy)²

6.4 — Credit Spreads & Term Structure

Credit spreads: yield premium over Treasuries for default risk. IG: 50–200bps. HY: 300–800bps (blew to 2000+ in 2008). Term structure: how rates vary by maturity. Inverted curve is one of the best recession predictors.

6.5 — VaR, Stress Testing & Drawdown

VaR: "5% chance of losing more than $X." Parametric: VaR₉₅ = −(μ−1.645σ). Historical uses actual returns. Monte Carlo simulates thousands of paths. All have limitations — VaR says nothing about severity beyond the threshold.

Stress testing: "What happens in a 2008 replay?" Endowments run 5–10 scenarios quarterly. Drawdown: Peak-to-trough decline. Max drawdown of 30% on $10B = $3B lost value.

  3. FI portfolio: $200M Treasuries (D=6.5), $100M IG Corp (D=4.2), $50M HY (D=3.1). Dollar-weighted duration? Loss if rates +1%?

Week 7Derivatives — Forwards, Futures & Options
QuantitativeApplied

7.1 — Forwards & Futures

Forwards obligate buying/selling at a future date and price. Futures are standardized, exchange-traded with daily settlement. Pricing follows cost of carry.

Futures PricingF₀ = S₀ × e^(r−q)T — S₀=spot, r=risk-free, q=dividend yield, T=time

Endowments use futures for overlay management — adjusting market exposure without trading underlying assets. If equities drift from 40% to 45%, sell S&P futures to rebalance without liquidating managers.

7.2 — Options

Calls give right to buy at strike; puts give right to sell. Asymmetric payoffs: limited downside (premium), potentially large upside. This makes options valuable for portfolio protection.

Option Payoffs & Put-Call ParityCall = max(Sₜ−K, 0) | Put = max(K−Sₜ, 0)
Put-Call Parity: C − P = S₀ − K×e^(−rT)

7.3 — Black-Scholes & Greeks

Black-Scholes prices European options using stock price, strike, time, rate, and volatility. You can replicate an option by dynamically trading the underlying — the option must cost as much as this replicating strategy. Greeks: Delta (price sensitivity), Gamma (delta's sensitivity), Theta (time decay), Vega (vol sensitivity).

7.4 — Endowment Use of Derivatives

Three main uses: (1) Rebalancing overlays via futures, (2) Currency hedging via forwards on international investments, (3) Tail risk protection via put options during uncertainty.

Week 8Alternative Investments & Private Markets
AlternativesEndowmentApplied

8.1 — Private Equity Structure

GPs raise capital from LPs (endowments, pensions) into closed-end funds (10–12 year life). Capital is called over 3–5 years as deals are found. Fee structure: "2 and 20" — 2% management fee + 20% carry above an 8% hurdle. Top-quartile PE returns 2–3× net; bottom-quartile may not return capital. The J-curve: negative early returns (fees + calls without exits) before turning positive as companies mature.

8.2 — Venture Capital

Early-stage, high-growth companies. Returns follow a power law — 1–2 out of 20 investments drive most of the fund's return. Stages: seed → Series A/B/C → growth. Yale was an early mover into VC, capturing transformative returns.

8.3 — Hedge Funds

Long/Short: Buy undervalued, short overvalued. Global Macro: Large directional bets (currencies, rates, commodities). Event-Driven: Mergers, bankruptcies, restructurings. Quant: Statistical models. Key terms: lock-ups (1–3yr), gates (redemption limits), side pockets (illiquid positions).

8.4 — Real Assets

Real estate (core/value-add/opportunistic), infrastructure (toll roads, utilities), natural resources (timber, farmland, energy). Provide inflation hedging and diversification from financial assets.

8.5 — Private Markets Metrics

IRR: Time-weighted annual return on cash flows. Gameable via credit lines. TVPI: (Distributions + NAV) / Capital Called. Simple "did I make money?" DPI: Cash returned / Called. "DPI is real, TVPI is a story." PME: Compares PE to investing same cash flows in a public index. PME > 1.0 = PE beat public markets. The gold standard.

8.6 — Alternative Risks

Appraisal smoothing: Quarterly valuations underreact to markets, making vol artificially low (~8–12% reported vs. ~25–30% true for PE). Overstates diversification. Vintage effects: 2006–07 vintages (deployed into bubble) underperformed; 2009–10 (deployed into crash) were exceptional. Endowments diversify by committing annually. Denominator effect: When public markets crash, PE doesn't mark down as fast → PE appears "overweight" → creates forced selling pressure.

Week 9The Yale Model — Endowment Portfolio Theory
EndowmentAppliedTheory

9.1 — Swensen's Philosophy

David Swensen managed Yale's endowment 1985–2021, generating ~13.7% annualized and growing it from $1B to $42B. Three pillars: (1) equity orientation for long-term compounding, (2) diversification across fundamentally different return drivers, (3) heavy illiquid alternatives where markets are inefficient. Endowments differ from most investors: perpetual horizon, no definite liabilities, sophisticated governance. This creates a structural edge.

9.2 — The Policy Portfolio

Yale's 2020 allocation: ~23% absolute return, 23% VC, 17% LBOs, 8% foreign equity, 2% domestic equity, 4% natural resources, 10% real estate, 8% FI, 5% cash. Only ~10% in liquid public markets — radical departure from 60/40. Designed around the principle that illiquidity, complexity, and access are rewarded over long horizons.

9.3 — Spending Rules

The fundamental challenge: intergenerational equity. Spend too much today → starve future students. Too little → fail current ones. Target: ~5% annual draw.

Yale Spending RuleSpendingₜ = 0.80 × Spendingₜ₋₁ × (1+π) + 0.20 × s × Vₜ
π = inflation, s = target rate (~5.25%), V = endowment value

The 80/20 weighting smooths short-term volatility — spending changes slowly even when markets crash, essential for university budget planning.

9.4 — Active Where It Matters

Active management is negative-sum in efficient markets (fees destroy value), positive-sum in inefficient ones. Strategy: index large-cap domestic equities; allocate aggressively to active in VC, PE, real assets. The endowment's edge is identifying top managers before funds fill — access is the scarcest resource.

9.5 — Governance

Yale's office: ~25 people, aligned compensation, long tenures. IC provides oversight without micromanagement. Swensen: governance determines outcomes — brilliant strategy fails with poor governance.

9.6 — Model Comparison

Yale Model: Heavy alts, active, concentrated relationships. Requires top-quartile access + governance. Norway Model (NBIM): Low-cost, passive, systematic factor exposure. 60/40: Simple, liquid, cheap — outperforms most endowments that lack governance to execute Yale Model.

Key Lesson

The Yale Model requires top-quartile manager access, patience through J-curves, sophisticated governance, and long-term commitment. Without these, simpler is better. Most universities that copied Swensen underperformed because they lacked institutional capacity.

  5. Anti-Swensen Argument: One page against the Yale Model. Consider: Harvard 2009 liquidity crisis, average PE/VC returns vs. public markets, fee drag, governance requirements. When should an endowment NOT follow Swensen?

Academic Foundations
Deep Dives: Theory & Concepts

Comprehensive reference for every concept you must master. Each includes explanations, formulas, real-world context, and practice problems.

Portfolio theory is the most important foundation — the language every endowment CIO, analyst, and consultant speaks.

Expected Return

For a portfolio: E(Rₚ) = Σ wᵢ×E(Rᵢ). This linearity is key — portfolio expected return is simply the weighted average. Risk is NOT linear, which is why diversification works.

Key FormulaE(Rₚ) = w₁E(R₁) + w₂E(R₂) + ... + wₙE(Rₙ)
Application

Endowment: 40% equities (8%), 20% bonds (3%), 25% PE (12%), 15% RE (7%). E(Rₚ) = 3.2+0.6+3.0+1.05 = 7.85%.

Variance & Covariance

Portfolio variance is NOT the weighted average of variances. The cross-terms (covariances) dominate. For N assets: N variance terms but N(N−1)/2 covariance terms. With 100 assets: 100 variances, 4,950 covariances. Correlations determine portfolio risk.

Why Correlations Matter More Than Volatility

100 stocks, each σ=30%. If ρ=1.0: portfolio σ=30%. If ρ=0.3: σ≈16.8%. If ρ=0.0: σ=3.0%. Same individual risk, dramatically different portfolio risk.

Diversification

Reduces risk without reducing expected return. Works because covariance terms reduce portfolio variance when ρ<1. Benefit diminishes after 15–20 uncorrelated positions. Breaks down in crises when correlations spike toward 1 — exactly when you need it most.

Efficient Frontier & MVO

The upper boundary of all possible portfolios in risk-return space. MVO finds it by minimizing variance for each target return. The tangency portfolio has the highest Sharpe ratio. In practice, MVO is fragile — use constraints, Black-Litterman, or resampled frontiers.

Sharpe Ratio & CAPM

Sharpe = reward per unit of total risk. CAPM: only beta is rewarded because idiosyncratic risk diversifies away. For endowments: don't pay for diversifiable risk; pay only for alpha or risk premiums you can't replicate cheaply.

Exercises
  1. Prove adding an asset improves the Sharpe ratio if its own Sharpe exceeds ρ × (portfolio Sharpe). What does this imply?
  2. Build a 5-asset efficient frontier in Excel (US Eq, Int'l Eq, EM Eq, Bonds, RE) with 20 years of data. Where does Yale's policy portfolio plot?

When endowment professionals say "risk," they mean many different things. You need each type.

Systematic vs. Idiosyncratic

Systematic: market crashes, rate changes, recessions — cannot be diversified, compensated via beta. Idiosyncratic: firm-specific — CEO scandal, drug trial failure. Diversifiable, uncompensated. Endowments minimize uncompensated risk across many managers.

Factor Risk

Decompose portfolio risk into factor exposures: market, size, value, momentum, credit, duration, currency. An endowment might find 80% of equity risk is market, 10% value tilt, 10% stock-specific. Reveals whether intended risks match actual risks.

Tracking Error

TE = σ(Rₚ−Rᵦ). A TE of 5% means the manager deviates ~5%/year from the index. Low TE = benchmark hugging (not earning fees). High TE = making active bets.

Value at Risk

"5% chance of losing more than $X in one month." Parametric (normal assumption, fast, underestimates tails), Historical (actual returns, captures fat tails), Monte Carlo (most flexible, computationally heavy). Biggest weakness: says nothing about severity beyond the threshold.

Parametric VaR (95%)VaR = −(μ − 1.645σ) × Portfolio Value

Stress Testing

Unlike VaR, stress tests ask: "What happens under specific scenarios?" Common: 2008 GFC (eq−40%, spreads+500bps), COVID (eq−34% in 3 weeks), Rising Rates (+300bps), Stagflation. Presented to IC quarterly.

Drawdown & Liquidity Risk

Max drawdown: worst peak-to-trough decline. For endowments, drawdowns breach spending floors and force selling at distressed prices. 60% illiquid allocation means you can't easily raise cash during crises.

Concentration Risk

Overexposure to single manager, sector, geography. Endowments track: max GP weight, sector tilts, currency exposure, manager overlap. Herfindahl Index = Σ(wᵢ²) quantifies concentration.

Exercises
  1. $5B portfolio, monthly μ=0.65%, σ=2.8%. Calculate 1-month 95% and 99% VaR in dollars. Annual 95% VaR.
  2. Stress test: 50% US eq / 20% bonds / 15% PE / 15% HF. "2008 replay" (eq−38%, bonds+5%, PE−25%, HF−19%). Total loss? Did VaR predict this?
  3. 25 PE managers, equal weight. Herfindahl? Now largest has 15%, rest share 85%. New Herfindahl?

Performance evaluation is the core daily work of endowment investment offices.

TWR vs. MWR

TWR chains subperiod returns, removing cash flow effects — measures manager skill. MWR/IRR weights by capital deployed — measures investor experience. Both are needed: TWR for managers, MWR for "did the endowment actually make money?"

Attribution

Allocation: (wₚ−wᵦ) × (Rᵦ,class − Rᵦ,total). Selection: wₚ × (Rₚ,class − Rᵦ,class). Every IC meeting decomposes: "Was it the tilt toward PE or the PE managers that drove performance?"

Risk-Adjusted Metrics

Sharpe (total risk), Information Ratio (active risk — best for benchmarked managers), Sortino (downside only), Treynor (systematic risk). Each tells a different story; know when to use which.

The Four Questions

(1) What did it return? (2) Compared to what? (3) How much risk was taken? (4) What drove the result? Answer these clearly and you're doing the job.

Exercises
  1. Fund starts $100M, returns 10% Q1, gets $50M inflow, returns −5% Q2. Calculate TWR and MWR. Why different?
  2. Policy 60/40, actual 70/30. Equity 12% (bench 10%), bonds 3% (bench 4%). Decompose into allocation + selection.
  3. Managers: A (α=2%,TE=3%), B (α=3%,TE=8%), C (α=1.5%,TE=2%). Rank by IR. Which to hire?

Even as a total-portfolio generalist, equity fluency is non-negotiable.

Market Cap

Price × shares. Large (>$10B), Mid ($2–10B), Small (<$2B). Endowments often overweight small/mid for alpha potential in less efficient segments.

Valuation Multiples

P/E (price per $1 earnings, avg 18–22×), P/B (book value, useful for financials), EV/EBITDA (capital-structure neutral). Used to assess market/sector cheapness.

Style Factors

Value (low P/E, high div), Growth (high revenue, high multiples), Momentum (past winners), Quality (high ROE, low leverage), Size (small-cap premium). Endowments structure equity by style bucket and monitor aggregate factor exposure to avoid unintended tilts.

Exercises
  1. Pull P/E, P/B, div yield for S&P 500, Russell 2000, MSCI EAFE, MSCI EM. Which is cheapest on each? Is cheap = good?
  2. Three managers: A (β_value=0.4), B (β_value=−0.2), C (market-neutral). Allocation 40/40/20. Aggregate value exposure? Target is 0.1 — does it match?

FI serves as both return source and risk-management ballast in endowment portfolios.

Yield

YTM: single discount rate equating price to cash flows. Bond market's IRR. "The 10-year is at 4.2%" = YTM on 10-year Treasury.

Duration

Modified Duration: % price change per 1% yield move. Duration 7 → loses ~7% if rates +1%. Longer maturity, lower coupon, lower yield = higher duration.

Convexity

Corrects duration's linear approximation. Positive convexity: gains from falling rates > losses from rising rates. Endowments seek it.

Credit Spreads

Yield premium over Treasuries. AAA: 30–80bps. BBB: 100–250bps. HY: 300–800bps. Widen in stress, compress in calm. Correlated with equity risk in crises.

Duration Contribution

Each position: weight × duration. Sum = portfolio duration. This is how endowments manage aggregate rate risk.

Exercises
  1. 5-year, 3% annual coupon at $96. Calculate YTM and Modified Duration. Price change if yields +75bps?
  2. FI portfolio: 60% Treasuries (D=7.2), 25% IG (D=5.1), 15% TIPS (D=4.8). Portfolio duration? Rebalance to target D=5.0?

Endowments allocate 40–60% to alternatives. This separates you from textbook-only finance.

PE: GP/LP Structure

GP manages, takes 2+20. LPs commit capital, receive calls over 3–5 years. Fund life 10–12 years. Understanding LPAs, cash flow modeling, commitment pacing is essential intern work.

Performance Metrics

IRR: Captures timing, but gameable via credit lines. TVPI: Intuitive multiple, ignores time (2.0× over 5yr is great; over 15yr is mediocre). DPI: Cash returned, most conservative — "doesn't lie." PME: Dollar-for-dollar vs. public index. Gold standard.

Appraisal Smoothing

Quarterly valuations underreact to markets. Reported PE vol ~8–12%; true economic vol ~25–30%. Overstates Sharpe ratios and diversification. Getmansky-Lo-Makarov (2004) provides unsmoothing methodology.

Vintage Effects & Pacing

Returns vary hugely by deployment year. 2007 vintages (bubble) underperformed; 2009 (crash) outperformed. Endowments commit annually for vintage diversification. Target: 3–5% of AUM per year to maintain steady-state allocation.

Exercises
  1. VC fund: Yr0 −$5M, Yr1 −$3M, Yr2 −$2M, Yr5 +$4M, Yr7 +$18M, Yr8 +$12M. IRR, TVPI, DPI. Top quartile if bench=2.5× TVPI?
  2. $8B endowment targeting 25% PE. Average fund life 12yr. Annual commitment needed to maintain $2B? What if commitments stop 2 years?
  3. Take quarterly PE returns. Apply unsmoothing. Compare smoothed vs. unsmoothed vol and correlation with equities. How does optimal alts allocation change in MVO?

Practical statistics you can apply to real portfolio data without making embarrassing errors.

Descriptive Statistics

Arithmetic mean (single-period) vs. geometric (multi-period). Fund gaining 50% then losing 50%: arithmetic mean 0%, geometric −13.4%. Skewness: negative = fat left tail (common in equity). Kurtosis: fat tails overall — extreme events more frequent than normal.

Regression

OLS workhorse: Rₚ−Rf = α + β(Rₘ−Rf) + ε. Alpha (intercept), beta (slope), R² (explained variance). Key diagnostics: t-stats (is alpha significant?), residual plots (assumptions violated?).

Correlation ≠ Causation

Two assets may respond to a common factor without causing each other. HF returns and M&A volume correlate — both increase in expansions, neither causes the other.

Annualization

Returns: (1+Rₘ)^12−1 (geometric) or ×12 (arithmetic). Volatility: ×√12. Assumes IID returns — autocorrelation (momentum/reversion) can make this over/underestimate.

Data Quality

Survivorship bias (only survivors, +1–3%/yr overstatement), backfill bias (report after good performance), stale pricing (low vol illiquids), look-ahead bias (using future info). All pervasive in alternatives.

Exercises
  1. 10 years monthly S&P 500: arithmetic mean, geometric mean, σ, skewness, kurtosis. Normal distribution? What does kurtosis tell you?
  2. Regress HF index on S&P. Report α, β, R², t-stat on α. Significant at 5%?
  3. Monthly S&P vol ≈ 4.5%. Annualize. Calculate rolling 12-month realized vol. When/why does it deviate from 4.5%×√12?
Endowment Theory
David Swensen & The Yale Model

Swensen didn't just manage a portfolio — he created a paradigm. Understanding his framework and its limitations is essential for any endowment career.

01

Equity Orientation

Perpetual endowments tilt toward equity-like assets. Equities compound wealth over decades in ways bonds cannot. Accept short-term volatility for long-term real growth above spending rates.

02

Broad Diversification

Spread risk across fundamentally different return drivers — equities, bonds, PE, VC, real assets, absolute return. True diversification means assets responding to different economic forces.

03

Illiquidity Premium

Endowments lock up capital for 10+ years, earning premiums inaccessible to banks, mutual funds, and individuals. Illiquidity is the cost; the premium is the reward.

04

Active Where Inefficient

Active management in inefficient markets (VC, PE, real assets) where information asymmetry creates alpha. Index where efficient (US large-cap). Don't pay active fees for passive returns.

05

Manager Access as Edge

Top-quartile PE/VC managers dramatically outperform median. Identifying, accessing, and building relationships with these managers is the endowment's competitive advantage.

06

Governance Excellence

Small, talented teams with aligned incentives. IC oversight without micromanagement. Process discipline, clear decision rights, long tenures. Poor governance destroys brilliant strategy.

07

Spending Discipline

Yale's 80/20 smoothing rule prevents panic spending in booms and catastrophic cuts in busts. The rule, not the market, drives the university budget.

08

Contrarian Rebalancing

Buy what's fallen, sell what's risen to return to policy weights. Psychologically difficult — buying equities in a crash — but mathematically correct. Rebalancing is a return source.

Swensen Exercises
  1. Compare Yale's 2020 policy portfolio to 60/40 over 20 years. Higher return, Sharpe, max drawdown? What did complexity buy?
  2. Harvard lost ~$11B (27%) in FY2009 — forced to sell, borrow, cut spending. Failure of the Yale Model or Harvard's execution? One-page post-mortem.
  3. Model Yale's spending rule in Excel. $30B, 8% return, 3% inflation, 5.25% target. 30 years. Then add 40% drawdown in Year 5. What happens to spending?
  4. Argue for and against: "Endowments under $1B should not follow the Yale Model." Consider access, governance, liquidity, fees.
